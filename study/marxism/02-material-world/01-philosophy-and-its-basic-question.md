Every part of this course rests on one choice: is the world, in the end, matter or mind?
This lesson sets up the language for that choice. It explains what philosophy is (a
worldview made systematic), how it differs from everyday experience and from the
sciences, and then the question Engels called the **basic question of philosophy**: the
relation between thinking and being. How a philosophy answers it sorts it into
materialism or idealism, and into knowability or agnosticism. Exams ask for these
definitions word for word, and later chapters (matter, consciousness, dialectics,
knowledge) all start from them.

## What philosophy means

The word comes from Greek: *philos* (loving) + *sophia* (wisdom). *Philosophia* passed
through Latin and Old French into English as *philosophy*, "the love of wisdom", and the
Chinese course glosses it the same way: {{zh 爱智慧——对智慧的追求}}. A philosopher is not
someone who owns wisdom, but someone who keeps pursuing it.

What does philosophy pursue wisdom *about*? About the world as a whole. Ancient Chinese
writers already had a word for "everything": {{term universe | 宇宙}}. The 《尸子》 says
{{zh 上下四方曰宇，往古来今曰宙}}: 宇 is space in every direction, 宙 is time past and
present. The 《文子》, 《庄子》 and 《淮南子》 say much the same, and the Buddhist
《楞严经》 explains the word 世界 the same way: 世 is time flowing (past, present, future),
界 is place (the ten directions). So the slides sum up: {{zh 世界是全部时间与空间的总称}}
— the world is the sum of all time and all space.

### Common sense, the sciences and philosophy

People grasp the world in three basic ways. The course compares them like this:

| Compared on | Experience and common sense 经验和常识 | Specific sciences 具体科学 | Philosophy 哲学 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What it studies | One particular thing or problem | The laws and secrets of one field or kind of thing | The essence and laws of development of the whole world, and humans' relation to it |
| Kind of thinking | Experiential thinking 经验思维 | Empirical, positive thinking 实证思维 | Theoretical thinking 理论思维 |
| What it gives us | Some knowledge for knowing and changing the world | Concrete methods for knowing and changing the world | Methodological guidance for knowing and changing the world |

What they share: all three are basic ways in which people grasp the real world
({{zh 都是人们把握现实世界的基本方式}}). The difference is scope: a farmer knows that
this field floods in spring (common sense); hydrology explains how rivers flood
(science); philosophy asks what the world as a whole is and how anything in it changes.

## Worldview and methodology

::: define Worldview | 世界观
People's general view of and basic standpoint on the world as a whole: their
fundamental answer to *what the world is* and *what it is like*.

> 世界观是人们对世界的总的看法和根本观点。
:::

Everyone has a worldview, usually without having thought it through. Someone who says
"everything happens for a reason, it's fate" and someone who says "it all depends on
what you do" already hold different worldviews.

A worldview is not only something you believe; you also *use* it, to look at problems
and to decide what to do. Used that way, it becomes a method.

::: define Methodology | 方法论
The fundamental principles and methods people use to know and change the world.

> 方法论是人们认识世界和改造世界的根本原则和根本方法。
:::

::: callout key Worldview and methodology are one thing seen twice
What you think the world *is* decides how you go about *dealing* with it. The course
puts it: a worldview, used to observe, think about and solve problems, becomes a
methodology. One worldview, one methodology: a materialist worldview gives the method
"start from reality", an idealist one "start from what you think".
:::

## How worldviews developed: myth, religion, philosophy

The course traces worldviews along a path: **myth → religion → philosophy**
({{zh 神话 → 宗教 → 哲学}}).

**Myth.** Pangu splits heaven from earth (盘古开天), Nüwa shapes people from clay
(女娲造人), Hou Yi shoots down the extra suns (后羿射日), Chang'e flies to the moon,
Kuafu chases the sun, the bird Jingwei tries to fill the sea with pebbles. In the
course's account, myth is the source of human culture: people changing the forces of
nature *in fantasy*, an art rooted in real life. Myths follow a certain logic of life
and contain some reasonable understanding (people do need fire, floods do need taming);
and their gods gave religion its raw material.

**Religion.** The course defines religion's worldview as one that people create
according to their own needs and imagination: an illusory, upside-down reflection in
people's minds of the natural and social forces that rule over them. (Buddhism,
Christianity and Islam are the slides' pictures.) The forces are real; the gods that
stand for them are the upside-down part.

**Philosophy.** A philosophical worldview comes from people's questions about the
universe and life, asked in the course of living and working, and it answers them
with reasons rather than stories. Its first great figures lived at roughly the same
time in China and Greece:

::: timeline Early philosophers on the slides
c. 571–471 BC | Laozi | 老子
551–479 BC | Confucius | 孔子
469–399 BC | Socrates | 苏格拉底
427–347 BC | Plato | 柏拉图
:::

## What philosophy is

::: define Philosophy | 哲学
A systematic, theoretical worldview.

> 哲学是系统化、理论化的世界观。
:::

Two words do the work. *Systematic*: its views hang together as a whole instead of being
scattered opinions. *Theoretical*: they are argued with concepts and reasons, not
told as stories or taken on faith. So every philosophy is a worldview, but not every
worldview is a philosophy — the slides draw philosophy as a small circle inside the
circle "worldview".

::: callout pitfall Worldview ≠ philosophy
"Everyone has a philosophy" is loose talk. Everyone has a **worldview**; only a
worldview raised to a system of theory is a **philosophy**. Exams test the direction:
philosophy is a kind of worldview (哲学是系统化、理论化的世界观), not the other way round.
:::

### Its place in human knowledge

The slides draw knowledge as layers. At the bottom are the objects: **nature**,
**society** and **human thinking**. Each has its group of sciences: the natural sciences
and technology (科学技术), the social sciences (社会科学) and the sciences of thinking
(思维科学). Philosophy sits on top, linked to all three by two-way arrows: it
**generalises** what the sciences find, and it **guides** them with a worldview and
method.

### Philosophy grew into its full form

Philosophy did not arrive complete. In the course's account, its content grew from
incomplete to complete, and only philosophy since modern times is a worldview in the
full sense. Engels, in *Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy*
(1886), says the basic question of philosophy was put quite clearly, and took on its
full meaning, only after Europeans woke from the long hibernation of the Christian
Middle Ages:

> 哲学基本问题，"只是在欧洲人从基督教中世纪的长期冬眠中觉醒以后，才被十分清楚地提了出来，才获得了它的完全的意义"。

::: example Think it through: how are science and philosophy related?
The teacher's discussion question (week 2): using your own experience and your major,
what is the relation between science and philosophy? Give an example.
== The difference ==
Use the comparison table. A science studies one field (physics: matter's motion at
different scales; computer science: computation); philosophy studies the world as a
whole and humans' relation to it. A science thinks *empirically*, by experiment and
proof; philosophy thinks *theoretically*, about the most general concepts (matter,
motion, cause, truth). A science gives concrete methods; philosophy gives
methodological guidance.
== The connection: sciences are philosophy's basis ==
Philosophy generalises the sciences. When physics found radioactivity and the electron
around 1900, the old idea that matter *is* the indivisible atom broke down, and
philosophy had to rethink what "matter" means (Lesson 2.2). The great discoveries of
19th-century science gave Marxist philosophy its natural-science basis (Lesson 1.2).
== The connection: philosophy guides the sciences ==
Every scientist works with a worldview, whether they notice it or not: that the world
exists outside us, that it follows laws, that those laws can be found. These are
philosophical assumptions. A good method — look at things in their connections, in
development, as a whole — helps in any field: an engineer debugging a system checks how
the parts connect, not just each part alone.
== An example from a major ==
A student of computer science might say: the field asks *how* to make machines
recognise images; philosophy asks *what* knowledge and consciousness are — and the
answer shapes how far we expect machines to go (Lesson 2.3 on AI).
== Model answer ==
科学与哲学既有区别又有联系。区别：具体科学研究世界某一具体领域的规律，属于实证思维，为人们提供具体方法；哲学研究整个世界的本质和一般规律，属于理论思维，为人们提供世界观和方法论指导。联系：二者都是人们把握现实世界的基本方式；具体科学是哲学的基础，哲学是对具体科学成果的概括和总结，科学的发展推动哲学的发展（如物理学新发现推动了物质观的变革）；哲学又为具体科学提供世界观和方法论指导。所以，学习科学要有正确的哲学指导，研究哲学也要吸收科学的最新成果。
:::

## The basic question of philosophy

Philosophy discusses a great many questions, but in the course's account one runs
through all of them and is the most important and most basic.

::: define Basic question of philosophy | 哲学的基本问题
The question of the relation between **thinking and being** (思维和存在), also put as
consciousness and matter, or spirit and nature.

> "全部哲学，特别是近代哲学的重大的基本问题，是思维和存在的关系问题。"
> ——恩格斯：《路德维希·费尔巴哈和德国古典哲学的终结》
:::

"Thinking" here means everything mental: consciousness, ideas, spirit. "Being" means
everything that exists outside the mind: nature, matter. The question has **two
sides**.

1. **Which is primary?** Is matter or consciousness the {{term origin of the world | 世界的本原}}: which comes first (第一性) and which is derived from it (第二性)?
2. **Can thinking know being?** Is there an {{term identity of thinking and being | 思维和存在的同一性}}: can our thoughts know the world, and know it correctly?

The first side splits philosophers into **materialists and idealists**; the second
into believers in **knowability** and **agnostics**.

## The first side: materialism and idealism

::: define Materialism | 唯物主义
The view that matter is the origin of the world and consciousness is derived from it:
matter comes first, consciousness after, and matter determines consciousness.

> 唯物主义认为物质是世界的本原，意识是派生的，先有物质后有意识，物质决定意识。
:::

::: define Idealism | 唯心主义
The view that consciousness (spirit) is the origin of the world and matter is derived
from it: spirit comes first and determines matter.
:::

Materialism and idealism are the **two basic camps** of philosophy
({{zh 唯物主义和唯心主义是哲学的两个基本派别}}). Each takes different forms: materialism
three historical ones, idealism two basic ones.

### Materialism's three historical forms

As times changed, materialism took different theoretical shapes. Lesson 2.2 follows
each one's idea of matter in detail; here is the map.

| Form | What it takes as the world's origin | The course's verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ancient naive materialism 古代朴素唯物主义 | One or a few concrete kinds of matter (water, fire, the five elements, atoms) | Right direction, but guesswork without scientific proof |
| Modern metaphysical materialism 近代形而上学唯物主义 | The atom: it mixes up the philosophical concept of matter with the physical one | Uses science, but one-sided; idealist about society |
| Dialectical materialism 辩证唯物主义 | Matter as a philosophical abstraction (objective reality), seen dialectically | The course's own position |

("Metaphysical" here is the course's word for a way of thinking that sees things as
isolated, fixed and unchanging; Lesson 3.1 explains it.)

### Subjective idealism

::: define Subjective idealism | 主观唯心主义
The kind of idealism that takes a person's own subjective mind (sensations, ideas,
will) as the starting point, and holds that the material world is an expression or
product of that mind.

> 主观唯心主义把人的主观精神（意识、观念等）作为认识世界的出发点，认为物质世界是人的主观意识的体现或产物。
:::

The slides give four voices:

- René Descartes: "I think, therefore I am" ({{zh 我思故我在}}): the one thing I can't
  doubt is my own thinking, so start from there.
- George Berkeley: "to be is to be perceived" ({{zh 存在就是被感知}}); "things are
  collections of sensations" ({{zh 物是感觉的集合}}).
- Lu Jiuyuan: "the mind is principle" ({{zh 心即是理}}).
- Wang Yangming: "there is nothing outside the mind, nothing happens outside the mind"
  ({{zh 心外无物，心外无事}}).

::: thinker George Berkeley | 贝克莱
dates: 1685–1753
works: *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* (1710)
An Irish bishop and philosopher, the textbook's standard case of **subjective
idealism**. He argued that for things, "to be is to be perceived" (esse est percipi):
an apple is nothing but the colour, taste and smell we sense, a collection of
sensations. The course takes this as making the world depend on the perceiving mind.
:::

Wang Yangming (1472–1529), who carried on Lu Jiuyuan's "school of mind" (心学), was
once asked about flowers blooming unseen on a mountain. He answered that before you
looked at them, they and your mind were both "still"; when you looked, their colours
became clear at once — so the flowers are "not outside your mind". The course quotes
{{zh 心外无物}} as Chinese subjective idealism. (Lesson 4.1 introduces him, with his
unity of knowing and acting.)

::: callout note Descartes on the slide
The slide lists Descartes with the subjective idealists, because "I think, therefore I
am" makes one's own thinking the starting point. Many textbooks call Descartes a
**dualist** instead: he held that mind and matter are two separate substances (dualism,
Lesson 2.4). If an exam asks which camp "我思故我在" belongs to, the course's answer is
idealism; if it asks about Descartes' view of substance, dualism.
:::

### Objective idealism

::: define Objective idealism | 客观唯心主义
The kind of idealism that takes some independent, objective spirit as the origin of the
world, and holds that the material world is an expression or product of this "spirit"
or "principle".

> 客观唯心主义把世界的本原归结为某种独立存在的、客观的精神，认为物质世界是这种"精神"或"理"的体现或产物。
:::

"Objective" here does not mean "true": it means the spirit is supposed to exist
*outside* any person's mind. The slides' three cases:

- Plato: the **world of Ideas** (理念世界). Real are the perfect, eternal Ideas
  (the Idea of a circle, of justice); things we see are imperfect copies. His allegory
  of the cave pictures it: prisoners chained to face a wall take the shadows cast by a
  fire for reality; one who escapes sees the real things in the sunlight. In the
  course's reading, the shadows are the material world, the things outside are the
  Ideas. (Lesson 4.1 returns to the cave.)
- Zhu Xi: **principle comes before things** (理在事先): the principle (理) of a boat
  exists before any boat is built.
- Hegel: the **Absolute Idea** (绝对理念), which develops itself and "goes out" into
  nature and history (Lesson 3.1).

::: thinker Zhu Xi | 朱熹
dates: 1130–1200
works: 《四书章句集注》 (commentaries on the Four Books)
The Southern Song thinker who completed Neo-Confucianism (理学). He taught that
principle (理) is prior to and more basic than material force (气): "before there were
heaven and earth, there was just principle". The course takes 理在事先 as Chinese
**objective idealism**.
:::

### Comparing the forms of idealism

| Question | Subjective idealism | Objective idealism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is primary? | The individual's own mind | A spirit outside any person (Idea, 理, Absolute Idea) |
| What is the world? | A product of my sensations and ideas | An expression of that objective spirit |
| Typical slogans | 存在就是被感知; 心外无物 | 理念世界; 理在事先; 绝对理念 |
| Thinkers on the slides | Descartes (as the slide places him), Berkeley, Lu Jiuyuan, Wang Yangming | Plato, Zhu Xi, Hegel |
| What both share | Spirit first, matter second | Spirit first, matter second |

::: callout pitfall Materialism vs idealism is not about money or ideals
In everyday Chinese 唯物 can sound like "materialistic" (caring about things) and 唯心
like "following your heart". In philosophy they mean only one thing: **which is the
origin of the world, matter or spirit**. A selfless revolutionary can be a
materialist; a greedy person can be an idealist.
:::

## The second side: can the world be known?

::: define Knowability | 可知论
The view that the world can be known: thinking can know being, and know it correctly.
:::

::: define Agnosticism | 不可知论
The view that the world cannot be known, or cannot be fully known.
:::

The course's case for knowability is Xunzi: {{zh 凡以知，人之性也；可以知，物之理也}}
— knowing is human nature, and being knowable is the nature of things.

For agnosticism it names David Hume and Immanuel Kant. Hume started modern agnosticism:
he doubted anything beyond our sensations, and denied that we can know objective laws
or necessary cause and effect in the outside world (we only see one thing follow
another, never the "must"). Kant held that we know only how things *appear* to us,
never the "thing in itself" (物自体). Kant returns in Lesson 4.1.

::: thinker Xunzi | 荀子
dates: c. 313–238 BC
works: 《荀子》 (chapters 天论, 解蔽, 礼论)
A Warring States Confucian with a strongly materialist streak: heaven (nature) runs by
its own constant course, "not for Yao, not against Jie". The course quotes him for
**knowability** (凡以知，人之性也；可以知，物之理也), for the active role of the mind
(Lesson 2.3) and for a materialist view of how things arise (天地合而万物生, Lesson 2.2).
:::

::: thinker David Hume | 休谟
dates: 1711–1776
works: *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40); *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748)
A Scottish philosopher, in the course's account the founder of modern **agnosticism**:
we know only our impressions, so we can't tell whether an outside world causes them,
and "cause and effect" is a habit of expectation, not a necessity we can know.
:::

Which camp holds which answer?

::: callout key Who believes the world can be known
**All materialists are believers in knowability**: matter comes first and consciousness
reflects it, so it can know it. **Thorough idealists are believers in knowability
too** — Hegel, for one — but for them knowing the world is spirit knowing itself, since
the world is spirit's product. Agnostics (Hume, Kant) stand apart from both.

> 一切的唯物主义者都是可知论者，他们坚持物质第一性，意识第二性。彻底的唯心主义者也是可知论者，但他们坚持意识第一性，物质第二性。
:::

::: callout pitfall Knowability does not mean materialism
"Believes the world can be known, therefore materialist" is a trap: Hegel believed the
world can be known and was an objective idealist. The two sides of the basic question
are separate: side one sorts materialism from idealism, side two knowability from
agnosticism.
:::

## The whole map, and why it matters

| The basic question 思维和存在的关系问题 | What it asks | The answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First side | Which is the origin of the world, which is primary: matter or consciousness? | Materialism 唯物主义 / idealism 唯心主义 |
| Second side | Is there identity between thinking and being: can thought know the world? | Knowability 可知论 / agnosticism 不可知论 |

Why call it *basic*? In the course's account, how a philosophy answers it is the
**premise and foundation for answering every other philosophical question**
({{zh 对哲学基本问题的回答，是解决其他一切哲学问题的前提和基础}}). The slides draw it as
a tree's roots. Ask "what is truth?": a materialist says thought matching objective
reality, an idealist something else. Ask "what moves history?": a materialist looks to
material production first. And a philosophy's answer to the first side decides which
camp it belongs to.

::: example Sorting statements by the basic question
Which camp does each belong to: "存在就是被感知", "理在事先", "天地合而万物生",
"凡以知，人之性也；可以知，物之理也"?
== Ask side one: what comes first? ==
"To be is to be perceived" puts the perceiving mind first: subjective idealism
(Berkeley). "Principle comes before things" puts an objective principle first:
objective idealism (Zhu Xi). "Heaven and earth unite and things are born" puts nature
first: (ancient naive) materialism (Xunzi).
== Ask side two: can it be known? ==
"Knowing is human nature, being known is the nature of things" says the world is
knowable: knowability (Xunzi).
== Conclusion ==
Read each statement with one question at a time; a statement may answer only one side.
:::

::: callout exam How this is examined
- Single choice: "the basic question of philosophy is…" → 思维和存在的关系问题;
  "the criterion dividing materialism from idealism is…" → which is primary (第一方面),
  **not** whether the world can be known.
- Sorting quotations into subjective idealism, objective idealism or materialism
  (心外无物, 理在事先, 存在就是被感知, 气者理之依也).
- True or false: "all materialists believe in knowability" (true); "all who believe in
  knowability are materialists" (false).
:::
