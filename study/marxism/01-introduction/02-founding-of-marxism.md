Theories don't fall from the sky. The course explains Marxism the way Marxism
explains everything else: by the material conditions, the social struggles and the
ideas that were there before it. This lesson answers the slide's question "why was
Marxism founded in nineteenth-century Europe?" in four steps — its **social roots**,
its **class basis**, its **intellectual sources** and the **personal conditions** of its
founders — and ends with its founding document, the *Communist Manifesto* of 1848.
Each step is a standard exam point, and the dates here are worth knowing exactly.

## The question of the age

Marx and Engels met in Paris in 1844 and wrote the *Manifesto* in Brussels in the winter
of 1847–48. The slide frames their situation as capitalism's "question of the age"
(时代之问): what did two young Germans face in mid-nineteenth-century Europe? In short:
an economy producing more wealth than ever, workers living in deeper misery than ever,
and workers' revolts that kept failing. Where is capitalism going, and where is
humanity going?

## Social roots

::: define Social roots of Marxism | 马克思主义创立的社会根源
The development of the capitalist mode of production in the nineteenth century. The
industrial revolution and technical progress raised labour productivity enormously; the
capitalist mode of production brought rapid growth of socialised large-scale production
and, at the same time, deep social disasters: **polarisation of society** and
**periodic economic crises**.
:::

Three things on the slides make this concrete.

1. **The industrial revolution.** Compare a hand-weaving workshop with a mechanised
   textile mill: one machine-tender could now produce what many weavers had produced
   before. Production became *socialised* — thousands of people working together on one
   product.
2. **Polarisation.** The new wealth went to factory owners, while workers — children
   among them — worked long hours in bad conditions for subsistence wages. The slide
   asks: faced with poverty spreading and deepening, where is the workers' way out?
3. **Periodic crises.** Every ten years or so production outran what people could buy,
   and the economy crashed: goods unsold, banks and firms bankrupt, workers thrown out.
   The slide calls capitalism a "monster": production grows, yet growth itself causes
   crisis.

::: timeline Economic crises of the nineteenth century
1825 | Britain's first general crisis of overproduction | 英国第一次全国性（普遍性）经济危机
1836–37 | Crisis in Britain and the US: banks contract, demand falls, firms go bankrupt (the slump lasted into the early 1840s)
1847–48 | Crisis that began with railway speculation and spread across Britain, France and the US
1857–58 | The first world economic crisis: bank failures spreading to every industry
1866–67 | Crisis in railways, shipbuilding and industry, with poor harvests
:::

::: callout note The slide's crisis dates
The slide gives the ranges 1836–1843, 1847–1850 and 1867–1868. Economic historians
usually date the crisis *years* as 1825, 1836–37, 1847–48, 1857–58 and 1866–67 (each
followed by years of depression). The pattern the slide points to — a crisis roughly
every ten years — is the same either way.
:::

## Class basis: the three great workers' movements

Who would answer the question of the age? The course's answer is the
{{term proletariat | 无产阶级}}, the modern working class that owns no means of
production and lives by selling its labour. Its struggle against the
{{term bourgeoisie | 资产阶级}} matured in three stages:

**smashing machines → economic struggle → political struggle**
(捣毁机器 → 经济斗争 → 政治斗争)

At first workers blamed the machines and broke them (the English Luddites of
1811–16). Then they fought the employers for wages and hours, with strikes and unions.
Finally they demanded political power. The banner: the proletariat moved **from
spontaneity to self-consciousness** (从自发走向自觉).

::: define Class basis of Marxism | 马克思主义创立的阶级基础
The struggle of the modern proletariat against the bourgeoisie, which by the 1830s–40s
had made the proletariat an **independent political force** on the stage of history,
above all in the three great European workers' movements.
:::

::: timeline The three great workers' movements
1831 and 1834 | Uprisings of the Lyon silk workers, France: first over wages ("live working or die fighting"), then with political demands | 法国里昂工人起义
1836–1848 | The Chartist movement in Britain: a national movement for the People's Charter (votes for working men), with mass petitions in 1839, 1842 and 1848 | 英国宪章运动
1844 | The Silesian weavers' uprising in Prussia (June), crushed by troops | 德国西里西亚纺织工人起义
:::

Why did all three fail? The slide's answer comes in two boxes.

| Aspect | What the course says |
| --- | --- |
| Significance (意义) | They marked the modern proletariat's arrival as an independent political force; they supplied the practical experience a scientific theory needed |
| Lessons (教训) | They lacked the guidance of a scientific theory; they had no leadership by a proletarian party |

::: law The workers' struggle needs scientific theory | 现代无产阶级的斗争迫切需要科学理论的指导
The struggle of the modern proletariat urgently needed a scientific theory to guide it.
Its failures came from lacking a scientific theory and lacking the leadership of a
proletarian party; Marxism arose to meet that need.
:::

**Methodological significance.** Practice needs theory, and theory comes from practice:
the workers' movements supplied the experience that Marxism generalised, and Marxism
gave the movement direction. The lesson the course draws for any great undertaking is
to combine **scientific theory with organised leadership**. (Lenin's "without
revolutionary theory there can be no revolutionary movement" makes the same point,
Lesson 1.3.)

## Intellectual sources

Marx and Engels didn't start from nothing either. The slide's map has three layers.

::: define Intellectual sources of Marxism | 马克思主义创立的思想渊源
**Direct theoretical sources** (直接理论来源): German classical philosophy, English
classical political economy, and British and French utopian socialism. **Natural-science
premises** (自然科学前提): the cell theory, the law of conservation and transformation
of energy, and the theory of biological evolution. **Other sources**: ancient Greek and
Roman philosophy, the thought of the Renaissance, and the progressive ideas of French
historians of the Restoration period.
:::

Marx and Engels took these over **critically** (批判地继承): keeping what was right,
rejecting what was wrong, and turning it into something new.

| Direct source | Key figures (dates) | What Marxism took | What it rejected |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| German classical philosophy (德国古典哲学) | Hegel (1770–1831), Feuerbach (1804–1872) | Hegel's dialectics, the {{term rational kernel | 合理内核}}; Feuerbach's materialism, the {{term basic kernel | 基本内核}} | Hegel's idealism; Feuerbach's metaphysical, contemplative materialism |
| English classical political economy (英国古典政治经济学) | Adam Smith (1723–1790), David Ricardo (1772–1823) | The {{term labour theory of value | 劳动价值论}}, on which Marx built the theory of surplus value | Treating capitalism as natural and eternal |
| British and French utopian socialism (英、法空想社会主义) | Saint-Simon (1760–1825), Fourier (1772–1837), Owen (1771–1858) | Their criticism of capitalism and their pictures of a future society | Their utopianism: no laws of history, no reliance on the proletariat |

::: callout note Fourier's dates
The slide gives Fourier as 1768–1830; Charles Fourier lived 1772–1837.
:::

::: callout pitfall Rational kernel or basic kernel?
Hegel gave Marxism its **dialectics** (the "rational kernel", freed from his idealist
"shell"); Feuerbach gave it **materialism** (the "basic kernel", freed from its
metaphysical limits). Swapping them is a classic trap. Hegel and Feuerbach are discussed
properly in Lessons 3.1 and 4.1.
:::

::: callout key Sources and components line up
Each direct source fed one component (Lesson 1.1): German classical philosophy →
Marxist philosophy; English classical political economy → Marxist political economy;
utopian socialism → scientific socialism. Lenin's 1913 article names them "the three
sources and three component parts of Marxism".
:::

## The three great scientific discoveries

Philosophy also needed new science. The slide's summary: from the middle of the
eighteenth century natural science moved **from collecting material to ordering it**,
rising from empirical description to theoretical generalisation; with this, the
**metaphysical way of thinking** (seeing things as isolated and unchanging) broke down,
and **dialectical thinking entered natural science**. (The slide illustrates it with a
quotation it attributes to Laplace: a scientist who only collects phenomena is a
"hothouse flower" who will never find nature's great laws.)

::: timeline The three great discoveries of the nineteenth century
1838–39 | Cell theory: Schleiden for plants (1838), Schwann for animals (1839) | 细胞学说
1842–47 | Conservation and transformation of energy: Mayer (1842), Joule's experiments (1840s), Helmholtz *On the Conservation of Force* (1847) | 能量守恒与转化定律
1850–51 | Clausius and William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) state it as a general law of nature
1859 | Darwin's *On the Origin of Species*: evolution by natural selection | 生物进化论
:::

- **Cell theory.** All plants and animals are built of cells and grow by the formation
  of new cells. Engels: the secret of how organisms arise, grow and are built was
  uncovered, and what had looked like a miracle now appeared as a *process* following a
  common law.
- **Conservation and transformation of energy.** Heat, mechanical motion, electricity,
  magnetism and chemical energy turn into one another in fixed amounts, and none is
  created or destroyed. Helmholtz's turn of question: not "how can we build a perpetual
  motion machine?" but "if perpetual motion is impossible, what relations must hold
  between nature's forces?"
- **Evolution.** Species vary, many are born and few survive, the struggle for existence
  selects the fittest, and life develops from lower to higher forms. Engels called it the
  most successful attempt ever made to prove the historical development of nature.

::: callout note Two dates on the slide
The slide dates Mayer's paper 1841: Mayer wrote a first version in 1841, but it was
rejected; his paper appeared in 1842. The slide also dates Laplace's book "1789
《天体力学原理》": his *Celestial Mechanics* appeared from 1799 to 1825, and the
quotation itself can't be traced to it, so treat it as an illustration.
:::

Why do these matter to *philosophy*? The slide sorts them under two words.

| What the discoveries showed | Discovery | Which link or development |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Connection (联系) | Synthesis of urea (Wöhler, 1828) | Between inorganic and organic matter |
| Connection (联系) | Cell theory | Between plants and animals |
| Connection (联系) | Conservation and transformation of energy | Between the forms of motion |
| Development (发展) | The Kant–Laplace nebular hypothesis | Of the heavens |
| Development (发展) | Lyell's gradualist geology | Of the Earth |
| Development (发展) | Darwin's theory of evolution | Of life |

In Engels's words (*Ludwig Feuerbach*, 1886), thanks to these discoveries we can show
the connections not only *within* each field of nature but *between* the fields, and
draw a nearly systematic picture of nature's interconnection from the facts of
science itself. Nature turned out to be connected and developing — which is exactly
what **materialist dialectics** says (Unit 3). Science supplied the evidence; Marxism
generalised it.

::: example A class poll: "Marxism arose in the 1840s"
The slide asks whether you agree that Marxism was produced in the 1840s.
== The course's answer ==
Agree. Marx's turn to materialism and communism came in 1844; Marx and Engels worked
out historical materialism in 1845–46 (*Theses on Feuerbach*, *The German Ideology*);
the *Manifesto* of February 1848 marks the founding.
== The nuance ==
"Founded in the 1840s" is not "finished in the 1840s". *Capital* came out in 1867,
1885 and 1894, Engels's late works in the 1870s–80s, and successors kept developing it
(Lesson 1.3).
== Why the 1840s ==
All four conditions met then: capitalism had shown both its power and its crises
(social roots); the three workers' movements had made the proletariat a political force
(class basis); the three sources and the scientific discoveries were available
(intellectual sources); and two exceptional people were ready to do the work
(personal conditions).
:::

## The founders: Marx and Engels

The fourth condition is personal: the founders' gifts, choices and partnership.

::: thinker Karl Marx | 马克思
dates: 5 May 1818 – 14 March 1883
works: *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*; *Theses on Feuerbach* (1845); *The German Ideology* (1845–46, with Engels); *The Poverty of Philosophy* (1847); *The Communist Manifesto* (1848, with Engels); *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859); *Capital* (vol. 1 1867); *The Civil War in France* (1871); *Critique of the Gotha Programme* (1875)
Born in Trier, Prussia, into a lawyer's family. Studied law and philosophy at Bonn and
Berlin, took a doctorate (1841), edited the *Rheinische Zeitung* in Cologne (1842–43),
then lived in exile: Paris, Brussels, and from 1849 London, where he died. In the
course's account he is the principal founder of Marxism, the author of its two great
discoveries (Lesson 1.1), and the reason the theory bears his name.
:::

::: thinker Friedrich Engels | 恩格斯
dates: 28 November 1820 – 5 August 1895
works: *Outlines of a Critique of Political Economy* (1844); *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845); *Anti-Dühring* (1876–78); *Dialectics of Nature* (1873–83, unfinished); *Socialism: Utopian and Scientific* (1880); *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884); *Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy* (1886)
Born in Barmen, Prussia, into a factory owner's family. Working in his family's firm in
Manchester showed him the English working class at first hand. Co-founder of Marxism
with Marx; after Marx's death he edited volumes 2 and 3 of *Capital* and explained and
defended the theory. *Anti-Dühring* is his systematic exposition of the whole Marxist
system — philosophy, political economy and scientific socialism. He called his own role
"playing second fiddle".
:::

::: timeline Marx and Engels: the main stations
1818 | Marx born in Trier (5 May) | 马克思诞生
1820 | Engels born in Barmen (28 November) | 恩格斯诞生
1835 | Marx's school-leaving essay on choosing a profession; he enters Bonn University
1842 | Marx edits the *Rheinische Zeitung* in Cologne; Engels goes to Manchester
1843 | Marx moves to Paris
1844 | *Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher* (February): Marx's turn is complete; Marx and Engels meet in Paris (August) and begin their lifelong partnership | 《德法年鉴》
1845 | Marx expelled to Brussels; Engels joins him; *Theses on Feuerbach*
1848 | *The Communist Manifesto* (February); revolutions; both return to Cologne
1849 | Marx settles in London (August); Engels follows in November
1850–1870 | Engels in Manchester, supporting Marx's work
1867 | *Capital*, volume 1
1870 | Engels moves to London
1883 | Marx dies in London (14 March)
1895 | Engels dies in London (5 August)
:::

::: callout note The "footprints" slides
Two slides that weren't shown trace Marx's and Engels's moves place by place. A few of
Engels's dates there are off (he went to Manchester late in 1842, not January, after
military service in Berlin; he moved to Paris in 1846); the timeline above uses the
usual dates.
:::

**The choice.** Both could have lived comfortably — a lawyer's son with a doctorate and
a manufacturer's son. The course stresses that they gave this up for the hard road of
revolution, moving from country to country, studying, and guiding the workers'
movement.

**The change of thought.** The young Marx mocked philosophers who roam the heavens
looking for a far-off unknown land; he wanted to grasp the everyday things of the
street. By the *Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher* of February 1844 he had completed a
double change of thought (思想的转变): **in philosophy from idealism to materialism, in
politics from revolutionary democracy to communism**. In 1845 he summed up the new
outlook in the eleventh thesis on Feuerbach, now carved on his grave in London's
Highgate Cemetery:

> 哲学家们只是用不同的方式解释世界，而问题在于改变世界。

"The philosophers have only interpreted the world in various ways; the point is to change
it." It is the shortest statement of Marxism's practical character (Lesson 1.3, Unit 4).

**The partnership.** From August 1844 the two worked together for forty years to found
and complete Marxism; after Marx's death Engels explained and defended it. Lenin wrote
that their friendship surpassed the most moving legends of human friendship.

**Why "Marxism" and not "Marx-Engels-ism"?** Engels answered this himself in
*Ludwig Feuerbach* (1886): he had taken some independent part in founding the theory,
but most of its basic guiding ideas, especially in economics and history, and above
all their final, sharp formulation, belonged to Marx. What he contributed, Marx could
have done without him, except in a few special fields; what Marx did, he could not have
done. "Marx was a genius; we others were at best talented." So the theory rightly bears
Marx's name. In letters he said the same — that he had played second fiddle and was glad
to have had so splendid a first violin as Marx, and that praise of his own work was
often exaggerated at Marx's expense.

::: callout key Two founders, one name
Marx and Engels are **co-founders** of Marxism. It bears Marx's name because, by
Engels's own account, Marx was its **principal** founder: the basic guiding ideas and
their final formulation were his.
:::

## The founding document: the *Communist Manifesto*

::: define Sign of the founding of Marxism | 马克思主义创立的标志
The publication of **the *Communist Manifesto*** (《共产党宣言》) in **February 1848**.
Written by Marx and Engels as the programme of the Communist League, it was the first
systematic statement of the principles of scientific socialism, and marks the birth of
Marxism.
:::

Before it come the works in which the new outlook took shape — *On the Jewish Question*
and the *Introduction* to the critique of Hegel's philosophy of right (1844), the 1844
Manuscripts, *The Holy Family* (1845), the *Theses on Feuerbach* (1845), *The German
Ideology* (1845–46), *The Poverty of Philosophy* (1847). After it come the works that
developed it: the London notebooks of the 1850s, the economic manuscripts of 1857–58 and
1861–63, *Capital* (1867, 1885, 1894), *The Civil War in France* (1871), *Critique of the
Gotha Programme* (1875), *Anti-Dühring* (1878), *Ludwig Feuerbach* (1886).

::: timeline Main works of Marx and Engels
1844 | Marx, *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts* (unfinished); Engels, *Outlines of a Critique of Political Economy* | 《1844年经济学哲学手稿》
1845 | Engels, *The Condition of the Working Class in England*; Marx, *Theses on Feuerbach* | 《英国工人阶级状况》《关于费尔巴哈的提纲》
1845–46 | Marx and Engels, *The German Ideology* | 《德意志意识形态》
1847 | Marx, *The Poverty of Philosophy*; *Wage Labour and Capital* | 《哲学的贫困》
1848 | Marx and Engels, *The Communist Manifesto* | 《共产党宣言》
1859 | Marx, *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (with its famous preface) | 《〈政治经济学批判〉序言》
1867, 1885, 1894 | *Capital*, volumes 1–3 (2 and 3 edited by Engels) | 《资本论》
1871 | Marx, *The Civil War in France* | 《法兰西内战》
1873–83 | Engels, *Dialectics of Nature* (unfinished) | 《自然辩证法》
1876–78 | Engels, *Anti-Dühring* | 《反杜林论》

:::

::: callout note Dates in the works table
The *Theses on Feuerbach* were written in 1845 but first published by Engels in 1888;
*Ludwig Feuerbach* appeared in a journal in 1886 and as a book in 1888. The slide's
"《黑格尔法哲学批判》1844" is the published *Introduction* (1844); the manuscript of the
critique itself dates from 1843.
:::

**What it says.** The *Manifesto* opens: "A spectre is haunting Europe — the spectre of
communism." All the powers of old Europe — the Pope and the Tsar, Metternich and Guizot,
French radicals and German police — had allied to exorcise it. Two things followed:
communism was already recognised as a power, and it was time for communists to publish
their own views against the "fairy tale" of the spectre. It has four chapters:

| Chapter | Title | Chinese |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Bourgeois and Proletarians | 资产者和无产者 |
| 2 | Proletarians and Communists | 无产者和共产党人 |
| 3 | Socialist and Communist Literature | 社会主义的和共产主义的文献 |
| 4 | Position of the Communists in Relation to the Various Existing Opposition Parties | 共产党人对各种反对党派的态度 |

It ends with the most famous slogan of the workers' movement: the proletarians have
nothing to lose but their chains, they have a world to win —
**"全世界无产者，联合起来！"** (Workers of all countries, unite!)

::: callout pitfall The founding sign versus earlier milestones
The *Theses on Feuerbach* contain the "germ" of the new worldview and *The German
Ideology* first set out historical materialism systematically — but the **sign of the
founding** (创立的标志) is the *Manifesto* of February 1848. And the work in which
Engels **systematically sets out the whole Marxist system** is *Anti-Dühring*, not the
*Manifesto* (in-class question).
:::
